## Current authored route — 22 September 2026

The [guided A1 course](course-chapters.md) has four chapters: the little post office; a stop at home; the market; and the delivery. They cover all ten A1 curriculum topics once, while reusing earlier language. Each checkpoint has three prepared variants with a written message, a separate spoken update and eight comprehension/response questions.

Early checkpoints are notes for Barsik. The learner’s original letter remains sealed until Chapter 4, where it invites the learner to meet the friends helping Barsik. The ending resolves a familiar place, route, weather plan and suitable reply. Selecting a reply checks intent; it does not certify speaking ability.

Chapter readiness uses two successful distinct tasks per topic at 70% or higher plus two activity families across the chapter. A current-chapter challenge permits early testing. An independent checkpoint pass needs 80%, both essential details, audio playback and no hints or transcript. Supported practice is welcome, but a supported checkpoint needs a new unassisted attempt using another variant to unlock progress. These pilot rules replace coin thresholds for the main guided route. Coins, game purchases, old journey receipts and FSRS remain intact.

The five-world route, coin thresholds and proposed final-letter design below are historical planning context. Where they differ, the authored four-chapter content and current course rules take precedence. Completing all four chapters unlocks the A2 guided level; no authored A2 chapters or CEFR qualification are claimed.

## 1. The product promise

**Barsik is a postcat making his first big delivery. He has a letter for you. You help him find his way through the world, learning Russian along the route. When he reaches your doorstep, you can decipher the letter yourself.**

Barsik carries the letter and travels; the learner guides his choices through the application. The learner's physical location is not part of the fiction. The destination remains the reader throughout the campaign. Its final language challenge resolves the promise made on the home screen.

Barsik is curious, friendly and determined. He can demonstrate Russian, ask for help with a decision and react to what happens. He has an immediate objective in each scene: find a street, follow a sign or catch a boat. Each activity used within a scene must help resolve that objective. His personality comes through small actions and dialogue, rather than explanatory lore.

The letter remains sealed during the journey. Familiar places and people establish why the message matters; its full contents are the ending. The three authored final variants are invitations from friends helping Barsik along the route, containing a place, time and practical arrangement. Their content and answer keys are in the [chapter catalogue](course-chapters.md).

## 3. Historical five-world proposal

The following table records the earlier five-world proposal. The current release uses the four A1 chapters described above. Neither sequence claims CEFR proficiency.

| World | Immediate objective | Language to introduce and revisit | Observable resolution |
|---|---|---|---|
| The little post office | Get directions and set off | Greetings, names, simple questions | Barsik knows his first stop and leaves with the letter |
| The market town | Find the right street and collect supplies | Numbers, food, places | He follows the right street and has what he needs for the next leg |
| The forest | Follow signs and ask the animals for help | Animals, colours, directions | He reaches the path out of the forest |
| The harbour | Find his boat and cross the water | Travel, time, weather | He boards the right boat and reaches the final stretch |
| Your doorstep | Deliver and understand the letter | Familiar vocabulary and sentence patterns from the journey | The learner opens the letter and deciphers its message |

Each chapter follows a repeatable flow:

1. **Arrive:** a short scene establishes the place and Barsik's immediate problem.
2. **Prepare:** introduce the useful words and expressions, with examples and optional sound.
3. **Use:** practise through existing flashcard, reading, sentence or writing activities. A story task asks the learner to use relevant language to help Barsik.
4. **Resolve:** show the concrete consequence of the completed task. The boat leaves, the sign makes sense or the route is found.
5. **Continue:** show coins actually earned and progress towards the next destination. Unlocked worlds remain available for revisits.

Ordinary practice can contribute coins without being rewritten as a fictional encounter. A story scene only claims a specific outcome when its relevant task has been completed. Every chapter contributes language towards the final letter; there is no unrelated surprise topic at the ending.

## 5. Historical final-letter design brief

The following design brief records the earlier proposal. The implemented [chapter contract](course-chapters.md) fixes the current content, eight-question format, support policy and pass rules; broader production and personalised lexical coverage remain future extensions.

### Purpose and sequence

**The final challenge asks: can the learner understand a new message made from Russian they have already practised?** It assesses comprehension, with optional production afterwards. Memorising the final passage beforehand would defeat that purpose.

The final world has four stages:

1. **Arrival:** Barsik reaches the learner's storybook doorstep with the same letter. The delivery itself has happened.
2. **Open:** present an illustrated envelope and the complete Russian letter. The learner can read at their own pace.
3. **Decipher:** answer questions about the message and, optionally, write a short reply. The passage stays available while answering.
4. **Discover:** show the understood message, the result and the ending. Offer focused practice or another attempt where needed.

Working arrival copy: **“Your letter is here.”** Opening action: **“Read your letter.”** The learner encounters the actual message before any questions.

The pilot should use one concise letter and a small set of meaningful questions. A starting design estimate is **40–70 Russian words and 6–8 questions**, untimed, with saved progress. Length, reading demands and success thresholds are proposals to test, not fixed requirements.

### What the learner deciphers

An invitation provides concrete, connected questions: who sent it, where the reader should go, when to arrive, what to bring and which description identifies the place. The learner reconstructs the message's meaning; they do not need to translate every word literally.

Use varied tasks where they clarify understanding: select a place on a small map, choose the stated time, identify an object to bring, or give a short answer. An optional one- or two-sentence reply can reuse the writing activity later; it is not a dependency of the first reading assessment.

### Fair content coverage

Each letter version includes a manifest of the vocabulary, expressions, tested meanings, grammatical forms and sentence patterns it uses. Link these to existing lexical/card/content IDs where applicable; do not add a universal English translation to the vocabulary table.

Every scored language target must have been introduced and practised before the assessment begins. Merely storing a word in the vocabulary library, generating a card or earning coins elsewhere does not establish coverage. Practised also does not mean mastered: the assessment is where independent understanding is measured.

At preparation time, choose a letter version whose language is covered by this learner's recorded activities. Explicitly gloss incidental names or untested language. Include question wording and distractors in the coverage check so difficulty is not smuggled in through the answers.

For the first release, use a small set of prepared letter variants with known language manifests and answer keys. These can be AI-drafted from the curriculum and edited when needed; the learner is not asked to write their own exam. If coverage is insufficient, name the missing preparation and offer a short practice set or a suitable simpler variant. Do not invent a supposedly personalised exam from unrelated vocabulary. Freeze the selected letter, questions, coverage evidence and assessment policy when the attempt starts; edits to cards or later practice must not change an active exam.

### Marking, help and retries

Assess meaning. English paraphrases and valid Russian alternatives should receive credit under a stored answer key/rubric. A wrong literal translation must be correctable as a content issue. The first release should use reliably marked selection and short-answer tasks; AI can assist content preparation or explanations, but an unavailable live model must not lose or prevent grading a supported exam attempt.

Keep the first submitted answer, support used, correction and final result distinct. Record help per question. A hint or answer reveal must not convert a later response into an independent correct answer. An example result is “You understood 6 of 8 details on your own, and 2 with help.” Reading with audio can be offered and recorded as supported reading; a separate listening score needs its own assessment definition.

A completed attempt always gets an honest result. Low scores lead to explanations and relevant practice, with another attempt available. A repeat attempt retains its own history and is labelled as a retry; where possible, use an equivalent covered variant. Coin totals and the already completed physical delivery are not reversed because of a low score.

Report independent comprehension, supported comprehension and concepts to revisit. The letter is considered deciphered once its essential message is understood, independently or with recorded help. A separate independent-pass result uses the versioned rubric. Define the essential details and proposed pass threshold when writing the pilot letter; do not silently treat any submission as a pass or require a perfect score by default.

The final screen can say **“You read your letter.”** when that outcome is supported, followed by what the learner understood and the story's invitation or reveal. It makes a concrete claim about this letter, not a general Russian proficiency certification.
